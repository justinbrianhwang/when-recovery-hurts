\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Recovery for learned policies}
Learned policies drift away from the states seen in demonstrations, and the resulting compounding error is the classical motivation for interactive imitation learning \cite{ross2011dagger,laskey2017dart}. Recovery methods return the system to familiar states: Back to the Manifold learns to steer out-of-distribution states back to the data manifold \cite{reichlin2022manifold}, POIR plans a return to the demonstration distribution \cite{cideron2023getback}, RecoveryChaining learns local recovery policies that hand control back to a nominal controller \cite{vats2025recoverychaining}, and Recovery RL restricts exploration with a learned recovery policy and recovery zones \cite{thananjeyan2021recoveryrl}. Recent work on vision--language--action and world--action models adds recovery at scale \cite{liufu2026repovla,li2026causalhistory}, retries after failure \cite{du2024toerr}, or rewinds to an earlier state \cite{zheng2026rewind}. These methods mainly report task success with and without recovery. Our question is narrower and complementary: for a given recovery intervention, how much of its net gain is rescue and how much is offset by harm on the same states, and can the harm be bounded before deployment.

\paragraph{Runtime monitoring and the value of acting}
Interactive imitation learning lets a human or a learned gate decide when to intervene \cite{kelly2019hgdagger,hoque2022thrifty}. Runtime monitors for generative policies detect or predict failures: Sentinel calibrates a temporal-consistency detector on successful rollouts and adds a vision--language progress check \cite{agia2025unpacking}, and FIPER calibrates out-of-distribution and action-entropy scores with conformal prediction \cite{romer2025fiper}; such calibrations control false alarms of the \emph{detector}. Language-model systems explain and correct failures \cite{liu2023reflect,duan2025aha}, and CheckVLA verifies action chunks with a world model \cite{liu2026checkvla}. A failure prediction does not say whether acting on it helps. RESCUE addresses exactly this question for vision--language--action monitors: it restores simulator and policy state at alarm checkpoints and compares alternative interventions from the same state, over a large number of counterfactual branches \cite{ahmedt2026rescue}. Our evaluation shares this same-state counterfactual design. It differs in scope (one hand-designed intervention studied end to end rather than a framework for many monitors), and it adds a pre-registered harm certificate on a calibration split, a single confirmatory efficacy test on a held-out split, and an audit of the controller's executed motion and of the physical record. A related concern about history-conditioned policies, whether memory that changes an action also guides it \cite{zhang2026memory}, applies to our memory ablation, which cannot isolate memory from a change in the confirmation rule.

\paragraph{Statistical risk control}
Learn then Test calibrates algorithms so that a chosen risk is controlled with finite-sample guarantees through multiple testing \cite{angelopoulos2021ltt}. Our certificate is the simplest instance of this idea: one pre-specified candidate, one exact binomial test per policy \cite{clopper1934}, and Holm's procedure across policies \cite{holm1979}. The efficacy test is the exact one-sided form of McNemar's paired test \cite{mcnemar1947}. None of these tools is new; the risk we control is the outcome harm of an intervention rather than a detector's error.
